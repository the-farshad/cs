**Discrete mathematics** studies structures that come in distinct, separate
pieces — integers, statements, steps of an algorithm — rather than continuous
quantities. It supplies the proof techniques that let us claim an algorithm is
*correct* for every input, not just the cases we tested.

the first domino; the inductive step guarantees each domino topples the next. Turn

## Proof by induction

To prove a statement $P(n)$ holds for every natural number $n$, induction needs two
parts:

1. **Base case:** show $P(1)$ is true.
2. **Inductive step:** assume $P(k)$ (the *inductive hypothesis*) and prove
   $P(k+1)$.

Together they cover every $n$ like falling dominoes. For example, to prove
$1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$: the base case $P(1)$ gives $1 = 1$, and
assuming $P(k)$,

$$1 + \cdots + k + (k+1) = \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1) = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2},$$

which is exactly $P(k+1)$. Induction is how we prove loops and recursive functions
work for inputs of any size.

## The pigeonhole principle

If you put $n+1$ pigeons into $n$ holes, some hole holds at least two pigeons. Stated
plainly: if $n$ items go into $m$ boxes and $n > m$, at least one box gets more than
one item. It sounds trivial but proves surprising facts — for instance, in any group
of 13 people, two share a birth month, and any hash table with more keys than buckets
*must* have a collision. More generally, some box holds at least
$\lceil n/m \rceil$ items.

## Solving recurrences

A **recurrence** defines a value in terms of smaller cases — exactly how recursive
algorithms describe their own cost. Merge sort, for example, satisfies

$$T(n) = 2\,T(n/2) + n.$$

**Unrolling** it builds a tree: each level does $n$ total work across its subproblems,
and there are $\log_2 n$ levels, giving $T(n) = O(n \log n)$. Other patterns:
$T(n) = T(n-1) + 1$ unrolls to $O(n)$, while $T(n) = 2\,T(n-1) + 1$ explodes to
$O(2^n)$. Recognizing a recurrence's shape is how you read an algorithm's running
time straight off its structure.

## Why it matters for CS

Induction proves that recursive algorithms and loop invariants are correct for all
inputs. The pigeonhole principle bounds what is possible — collisions, duplicates,
worst cases. Solving recurrences turns a recursive definition into a concrete
complexity like $O(n \log n)$. These three tools are how computer scientists reason
rigorously instead of merely testing.

## Takeaways

- Induction proves $P(n)$ for all $n$ via a base case plus an inductive step — the
  domino chain.
- The pigeonhole principle forces a collision whenever items outnumber boxes.
- Unrolling a recurrence into a tree reveals an algorithm's running time, like
  $O(n \log n)$ for $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n$.
