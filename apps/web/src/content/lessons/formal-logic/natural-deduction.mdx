Truth tables tell you *that* an argument is valid; **natural deduction** shows
*why*, by deriving the conclusion from the premises one justified step at a time.
A derivation establishes $P_1, \dots, P_n \vdash C$ — "$C$ is **provable** from the
premises." (Soundness and completeness guarantee $\vdash$ and $\models$ pick out
exactly the same valid arguments.)

## Rules of inference

Each connective comes with an **introduction** rule (how to derive a formula with
that main connective) and an **elimination** rule (how to use one). Modern names on
the left; Teller's primer writes $\land$ as $\&$ and $\rightarrow$ as $\supset$.

- **$\land$I / $\land$E** — from $X$ and $Y$ infer $X \land Y$; from $X \land Y$ infer either part.
- **$\rightarrow$E** (modus ponens) — from $X \rightarrow Y$ and $X$ infer $Y$.
- **$\lor$I** — from $X$ infer $X \lor Y$. **$\lor$E** (proof by cases) — see below.
- **$\leftrightarrow$I / $\leftrightarrow$E** — build/break a biconditional via the two conditionals.
- **Reiteration** — copy an earlier available line.

## Subderivations

The powerful rules open a **subderivation** — an indented box (the vertical bars in
the visualizer) that begins with an **assumption** you are free to make
*temporarily*. Two subderivation rules do most of the work:

- **Conditional proof ($\rightarrow$I):** assume $X$; if you can derive $Y$ inside
  the box, then $X \rightarrow Y$ holds *outside* it (the assumption is
  **discharged**). The default proof above uses exactly this.
- **Reductio / negation introduction ($\lnot$I):** assume $X$; if you derive a
  contradiction ($\bot$), conclude $\lnot X$. Switch the visualizer to "Modus
  tollens" to watch a reductio.

The one rule: a line inside a box may use earlier lines from enclosing boxes, but
once a box closes, its interior lines are **sealed** — you can only use what the
subderivation rule hands back out.

## Strategy

Work **backwards from the goal**: to prove a conditional, plan a conditional proof;
to prove a negation, plan a reductio; to prove a conjunction, prove each part. Then
work **forwards** from the premises to meet in the middle. The "Proof by cases" and
"De Morgan" examples in the visualizer show these patterns on longer derivations.

## Takeaways

- A derivation proves $P_1,\dots,P_n \vdash C$ with justified steps; $\vdash$ and $\models$ agree (soundness + completeness).
- Each connective has an introduction and an elimination rule.
- Subderivations make a temporary assumption that must be **discharged** — by conditional proof ($\rightarrow$I) or reductio ($\lnot$I).
- Strategy: plan backwards from the goal, derive forwards from the premises.
